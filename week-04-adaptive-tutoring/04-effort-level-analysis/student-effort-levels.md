# Week 4: Student Effort Levels

## Purpose

The purpose of this document is to analyze how much effort a student has shown before asking the tutor for help.

Adaptive tutoring should consider not only what the student is asking, but also how much thinking, work, or evidence the student has already provided.

The main question is:

How should the tutor's support change as student effort increases?

---

# Why Student Effort Matters

Two students may ask for help on the same programming topic but need very different responses.

For example:

Student A:  
"Solve this."

Student B:  
"I created a superclass and three subclasses, but I am not sure whether this method belongs in the superclass or subclasses."

Both students are asking for help, but Student B has shown more effort and more specific thinking. The tutor should respond differently.

A low-effort request should receive less direct help and more reflection. A high-effort request can receive more targeted guidance because the student has already engaged with the problem.

---

# Effort Level 1: Low Effort

## Description

The student gives little or no evidence of thinking, work, or attempted solution.

## Example Questions

- Solve this.
- Give me the answer.
- Write my homework.
- What is the code?
- Do this for me.

## Tutor Response Strategy

The tutor should not provide a full answer or complete code. It should redirect the student toward learning.

The tutor can say that it cannot complete the assignment, then offer to:

- Explain the relevant concept.
- Help break the problem into steps.
- Ask what the student has tried.
- Provide a small conceptual hint.

## Appropriate Support Level

Level 1: Reflection Prompt  
or  
Level 2: Conceptual Hint

## Educational Reasoning

Low-effort requests create a high academic-integrity risk. If the tutor gives too much help, it may replace the student's learning process.

The goal is to encourage the student to begin thinking.

---

# Effort Level 2: Moderate Effort

## Description

The student shows some thinking but has not provided enough detail for targeted technical guidance.

## Example Questions

- I think inheritance might help here, but I am not sure.
- I know I need a constructor, but I do not know what it should initialize.
- I tried using an ArrayList, but I am confused.
- I think my class design is wrong.

## Tutor Response Strategy

The tutor should ask the student to explain their reasoning and provide a conceptual or strategic hint.

The tutor can ask:

- What classes are involved?
- What relationship do you see between them?
- What have you tried so far?
- What output or behavior did you expect?
- Which part is confusing?

## Appropriate Support Level

Level 2: Conceptual Hint  
or  
Level 3: Strategic Guidance

## Educational Reasoning

Moderate effort shows that the student is engaging with the problem. The tutor can provide more support than it would for a direct solution request, but it should still avoid giving the final answer.

The goal is to help the student organize their thinking.

---

# Effort Level 3: High Effort

## Description

The student shows clear work, reasoning, or evidence. They may provide code, an error message, a design attempt, or a description of what they already tried.

## Example Questions

- I created an abstract superclass and three subclasses, but my overridden methods are not behaving correctly.
- I wrote my constructor, but my fields are still null after I create the object.
- My loop works for most cases, but it fails on the last element.
- I tested my method with two inputs, and one case fails.
- I think the problem is my method signature, but I am not sure.

## Tutor Response Strategy

The tutor can provide more specific guidance because the student has already done meaningful work.

The tutor can:

- Identify the likely concept involved.
- Suggest a specific next check.
- Ask the student to compare expected and actual behavior.
- Explain why the issue may be happening.
- Give a targeted hint without rewriting the full solution.

## Appropriate Support Level

Level 4: Targeted Technical Hint  
or  
Level 5: Structural Guidance or Pseudocode

## Educational Reasoning

High effort suggests that the student is actively learning and may benefit from more detailed feedback. The tutor can give stronger guidance while still leaving the final reasoning and implementation to the student.

The goal is to reward productive struggle and help the student move forward.

---

# Effort Level 4: Non-Graded Learning Request

## Description

The student is not asking for help on graded work. They are asking for a general example or explanation to understand a concept.

## Example Questions

- Can you show a small non-homework example of method overriding?
- Can you make up a simple example of polymorphism?
- Can you explain ArrayLists with a practice example?
- Can we walk through a practice problem?

## Tutor Response Strategy

The tutor may provide a small worked example if it is clearly separate from graded work and appropriate under course policy.

The tutor should still ask the student to explain the concept afterward.

## Appropriate Support Level

Level 6: Worked Example for Non-Graded Content

## Educational Reasoning

Worked examples can support learning when they are not replacing student work. They can help students see how a concept works in context.

The goal is to demonstrate a concept safely.

---

# Summary Table

| Effort Level | Evidence From Student | Risk | Tutor Response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low Effort | No attempt or asks for answer | High | Refuse full solution, ask reflection question |
| Moderate Effort | Some idea or partial reasoning | Medium | Give conceptual hint or strategic guidance |
| High Effort | Code, error, design, or specific attempt | Lower | Give targeted technical hint |
| Non-Graded Learning | Clearly asks for practice/example | Lower if safe | May give small worked example |

---

# Main Takeaway

Adaptive tutoring should respond to student effort.

When students show little effort, the tutor should avoid direct answers and encourage them to begin reasoning. When students show meaningful effort, the tutor can provide more specific guidance because they are already engaged in learning.

The goal is not to punish low-effort students. The goal is to protect learning by giving the right amount of support at the right time.
